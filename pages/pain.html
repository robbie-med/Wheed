Pain

Cannabis and pain — the more complicated story.

Cannabis is one of the most common reasons adults give for using marijuana, and one of the most heavily marketed indications at dispensaries. The actual evidence is more modest than the marketing — and chronic daily use can quietly make pain worse.

What the evidence says, in plain terms

The most rigorous reviews of cannabis for pain (NASEM 2017; Whiting et al., JAMA 2015; Mücke et al., Cochrane 2018; IASP position statement 2021) converge on a similar picture:

Modest evidence

Chronic neuropathic pain

Multiple RCTs show small to moderate improvements with cannabinoids for neuropathic pain (diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, MS-related pain). Effect size is similar to or smaller than first-line drugs like gabapentinoids.

Modest evidence

MS spasticity-related pain

Nabiximols (Sativex, a THC/CBD oromucosal spray) is approved in many countries specifically for MS spasticity. Real effect, modest magnitude.

Weak evidence

Generic "chronic pain"

Aggregate trial data shows small benefits but high heterogeneity. The 2021 IASP review explicitly concluded that current evidence does not support cannabinoid-based medicines as a general treatment for chronic pain.

Very weak / mixed

Fibromyalgia

Small studies suggest possible benefit. Larger, higher-quality trials are inconsistent. Many patients report subjective improvement; objective measures are harder to move.

Inadequate evidence

Acute pain (injuries, post-op)

Trial data does not support cannabis for acute pain. Some studies suggest it may actually worsen post-operative pain ratings.

Inadequate evidence

Headache, migraine, back pain

Anecdotal use is widespread; rigorous evidence is limited and often shows no benefit beyond placebo.

The hyperalgesia problem

This is the part most people haven't heard. Long-term, daily cannabis use can produce cannabinoid-induced hyperalgesia — a state in which the same painful stimulus is rated as more painful, even when the user is not currently high.

This is analogous to the well-documented phenomenon of opioid-induced hyperalgesia. The mechanism appears to involve adaptive changes in descending pain modulation and altered processing in the periaqueductal gray. The result: chronic use can produce the very thing it was being used to treat.

In experimental human studies, chronic heavy cannabis users have lower pain thresholds and higher pain ratings to standardized stimuli than non-users (Cooper & Haney, Drug and Alcohol Dependence 2016; subsequent replications).

!

The pattern many patients describe: "It used to really help my pain. Now I have to use a lot, and I'm in more pain than ever, but if I don't use I feel awful."

This is not weakness, and not imagination. It's a recognized pharmacological pattern: tolerance to the analgesic effect, plus dependence, plus possibly hyperalgesia. The way out is, frustratingly, the same as the cause.

The coping question

A separate concern, distinct from the pharmacology: cannabis is very effective at making pain matter less in the moment, without changing the underlying tissue or process. This is fine for short-term use. For long-term pain, it can be a problem because it crowds out other coping that does change the underlying process — exercise, physical therapy, sleep, treating depression and anxiety, addressing the source.

This is not unique to cannabis (the same critique applies to opioids, alcohol, and benzodiazepines for pain). But it's especially common with cannabis because the perceived side-effect burden is low and the access is easy. Years can pass before someone realizes that the strategy has become the problem.

Specific situations

If you're using cannabis for sleep because of pain

This is one of the most common scenarios. Cannabis helps you fall asleep; pain isn't treated; pain wakes you up later; you use more. Over time, sleep architecture worsens (see sleep), which itself amplifies pain perception. Treating the underlying pain and the sleep separately almost always works better than letting cannabis attempt both.

If you're using cannabis to taper off opioids

The evidence here is genuinely mixed. Some observational data suggests cannabis access reduces opioid prescribing at a population level. Individual studies of opioid users adding cannabis show inconsistent benefits. This is a conversation to have with a clinician who knows your specific case — not a generic recommendation.

If you're using cannabis for fibromyalgia or other central sensitization syndromes

Many patients report meaningful benefit, especially with balanced THC:CBD products. Trial data is weaker than patient enthusiasm would suggest, but the gap may reflect the difficulty of measuring fibromyalgia in trials rather than absence of effect. If it works for you and use is stable, that's reasonable data.

If your pain is acute, post-surgical, or post-injury

The evidence does not support cannabis here. It may interact with anesthesia (cannabis users often require higher doses of induction agents) and may worsen post-op pain ratings.

What a useful pain conversation looks like

If you're using cannabis for pain, the questions that matter are not "is cannabis good or bad?" They are:

  1. Is my pain actually better than it was a year ago?
  2. Am I using more product, more often, to get the same effect?
  3. Have I added or strengthened any non-cannabis pain strategies in the past year?
  4. If I stop for a week, does my baseline pain get worse, stay the same, or get better?

That last question is the cleanest test. If a week off makes pain worse, you may be treating real pain. If a week off makes pain better (after the first few days), you may have been treating tolerance.
