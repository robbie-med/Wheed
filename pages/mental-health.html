Anxiety, depression, and psychosis risk.

This is the area with the largest gap between what's culturally believed and what the evidence shows. The honest version is more nuanced than either "it cures my anxiety" or "it causes schizophrenia."

Anxiety

The cannabis–anxiety relationship is genuinely bidirectional, which is part of why it's so confusing.

Short-term, low-dose:

Acute, low-dose THC reduces anxiety in many people, often quickly. This is what most users report and is consistent with controlled studies. CBD by itself also has plausible anxiolytic effects, with cleaner trial data than for THC.

High-dose:

The dose-response curve for THC and anxiety is biphasic and inverts: low doses are anxiolytic, higher doses are anxiogenic. Crossing that threshold produces acute anxiety, panic, racing thoughts, derealization, and sometimes outright panic attacks. With today's high-potency products, that threshold is easy to cross by accident — especially with edibles.

Chronic use:

Longitudinal cohort studies consistently find that regular cannabis use is associated with higher rates of anxiety disorders over time, particularly generalized anxiety and social anxiety (Kedzior & Laeber, BMC Psychiatry 2014 meta-analysis; Twomey, Substance Use & Misuse 2017). Causality is debated — some people may use cannabis to self-medicate emerging anxiety — but the trajectory is real.

The clinically common pattern: someone uses cannabis to manage anxiety, develops tolerance, escalates dose or frequency, develops withdrawal-mediated baseline anxiety between doses, and ends up with anxiety symptoms that exceed where they started. Withdrawal anxiety is then attributed to the original disorder, reinforcing the belief that cannabis is helping.

→

A cleaner test: what happens to your anxiety 2 to 4 weeks after stopping? If your anxiety is significantly better after the withdrawal period passes, much of what you were treating was withdrawal. If it's the same or worse, you likely have a genuine anxiety disorder that deserves its own treatment.

Depression

Cohort studies show a consistent association between regular cannabis use and depression. The Lev-Ran 2014 meta-analysis (Psychological Medicine) found roughly 1.6× higher odds of depression among regular users. The arrow of causality is debated; both directions likely operate.

What's clearer: heavy cannabis use during depression treatment is associated with worse outcomes — slower response to antidepressants, more residual symptoms, more relapse (Bahorik et al., Drug and Alcohol Dependence 2017). For someone in active treatment for depression, cutting cannabis back is one of the more impactful single changes available.

Psychosis: the big risk

This is the area where the evidence is strongest and the implications most serious. Several lines of research converge on the same conclusion: regular use of high-potency cannabis substantially increases the risk of psychotic disorders, particularly in younger users and those with family or genetic risk.

What the data shows

  • The Swedish conscript cohort (Andréasson 1987, followed for 40+ years) showed a dose-dependent ~2–6× increased risk of schizophrenia in heavy adolescent cannabis users.
  • The EU-GEI multi-site case-control study (Di Forti, Lancet Psychiatry 2019): daily users of high-potency cannabis (>10% THC) had ~5× higher odds of first-episode psychosis compared to non-users. In Amsterdam and London — where high-potency products dominate the market — they estimated that 30–50% of new psychosis cases were attributable to cannabis.
  • Cannabis-induced psychosis (acute, requires hospitalization) carries a ~30–45% risk of converting to a schizophrenia spectrum disorder within 8 years (Starzer et al., American Journal of Psychiatry 2018).
  • The risk is concentrated in: young age at first use, daily use, high-THC products, family history of psychosis, and certain genetic variants (AKT1, COMT).

This doesn't mean most users develop psychosis. It does mean the population-level risk is real, well-replicated, and that for someone with a personal or family history of psychotic illness, heavy cannabis use is one of the most consequential modifiable risk factors in psychiatry.

!

If you've ever had a psychotic episode — heard or seen things others didn't, experienced strong paranoid beliefs, lost touch with reality — even briefly, even years ago, even just on cannabis: continued cannabis use is a serious clinical risk. This is one of the few situations where the evidence is clear enough to be directive. Talk to a clinician.

Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder vs. acute intoxication

Most acute psychiatric reactions to cannabis are acute intoxication — panic, paranoia, dissociation — and resolve as the drug wears off. They are not psychotic disorders, even though they can feel like one in the moment.

A subset of users develop a persistent psychotic state that outlasts intoxication (days to weeks). This is the higher-risk presentation — it predicts conversion to a chronic psychotic disorder in a substantial minority.

The most common scenario

The patient who uses daily, has gradually escalating anxiety, has had one or two "weird" episodes on edibles, and isn't sure whether their anxiety is the disease or the drug. The answer is almost always both, in proportions that can only be figured out by stopping for a few weeks and seeing what's left.

That's not a moral instruction. It's a diagnostic one.
