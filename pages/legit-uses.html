Where it does help

Cannabis has real medical uses. Here's the honest list.

Dispensary marketing implies cannabis helps with everything. It doesn't. But it does help with some specific conditions, with varying levels of evidence, and those uses deserve to be acknowledged clearly — not buried under the warnings.

Strong evidence

Refractory chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting

Synthetic THC analogs (dronabinol, nabilone) have been FDA-approved since the 1980s, specifically for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting in patients who have failed to respond to conventional antiemetics. Modern first-line antiemetics (5-HT3 receptor antagonists like ondansetron, NK1 antagonists like aprepitant, plus corticosteroids) have largely supplanted cannabinoids as initial therapy, but the refractory-CINV indication remains valid.

Specific pediatric epilepsies (Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut)

A purified CBD preparation (Epidiolex) is FDA-approved for these two rare, severe childhood epilepsies, plus tuberous sclerosis complex. Trial data is robust: meaningful reductions in seizure frequency in conditions that often don't respond to anything else. Notably, this is CBD specifically, not THC, and not general "medical cannabis."

Multiple sclerosis spasticity

Nabiximols (Sativex), a 1:1 THC:CBD oromucosal spray, is approved in many countries for moderate-to-severe MS spasticity that hasn't responded to other treatments. Effect size is moderate, real, and replicated.

Moderate evidence

Chronic neuropathic pain

Multiple RCTs support a modest analgesic effect for cannabinoids in neuropathic pain (diabetic, post-herpetic, MS-related, HIV-related). Effects are smaller than first-line agents like gabapentinoids and antidepressants, but real. May be a reasonable add-on in refractory cases.

HIV/AIDS-related cachexia and chemotherapy-related appetite loss

Dronabinol is FDA-approved for AIDS-related anorexia. Some evidence supports appetite stimulation in cancer patients, though newer agents have largely supplanted cannabinoids for this indication.

Limited but suggestive evidence

Sleep, in the short term

For acute sleep problems, low-dose cannabis can be effective. Long-term, the tradeoffs (tolerance, REM suppression, withdrawal-mediated insomnia) usually overtake the benefit. See the sleep page.

PTSD — specific symptoms

Patient-reported data and small trials suggest benefit for sleep and nightmares in some PTSD patients. Larger, controlled studies are inconsistent. The VA does not currently endorse cannabis for PTSD as standard care, but recognizes it as an active area of research.

Anxiety — CBD specifically, in some contexts

Small trials of purified CBD show anxiolytic effects (e.g., public-speaking-induced anxiety, social anxiety). This is not the same as the THC-dominant products typically sold for "anxiety relief," which tend to worsen anxiety long-term.

Conditions where evidence is genuinely weak, despite marketing

Cannabis is widely marketed for these conditions, and many users report benefit, but controlled trial evidence is thin or absent:

  • Generic "chronic pain" (back pain, headaches, joint pain)
  • Generalized anxiety disorder (THC-containing products specifically)
  • Depression
  • ADHD
  • Insomnia (long-term)
  • Glaucoma (real but tiny effect, brief duration, not clinically useful)
  • Cancer treatment ("cannabis kills cancer" is not supported by human evidence)
  • Alzheimer's, autism, ALS

This doesn't mean cannabis doesn't help some people with these conditions. It means we don't have rigorous evidence at the population level, and patient-reported benefits can include placebo effect, regression to the mean, and withdrawal relief from the previous dose.

An important point about "I tried it and it works"

Personal experience is real data — for you. It's also subject to several well-documented biases: placebo effect (large for cannabis because the subjective experience is salient), the post-hoc fallacy, the comparison to your worst week instead of your average, and the difficulty of separating drug effect from withdrawal relief.

None of this means your experience is wrong. It does mean that "I tried it and it worked" is not a substitute for a controlled trial, and that patients who try cannabis for ADHD/depression/anxiety and feel better should not assume cannabis is treating the condition rather than masking or substituting for it.

The honest summary

Cannabis has a small but real set of indications with strong evidence, a larger set with moderate evidence, and a much larger set with weak or no evidence. For a patient with MS spasticity, severe pediatric epilepsy, refractory chemotherapy nausea, or refractory neuropathic pain — cannabis is a serious treatment option, sometimes the best one.

For most other conditions, the dispensary's certainty exceeds the evidence base. That doesn't mean cannabis won't help you. It means the case is weaker than you've been told, and any benefit needs to be weighed against the downsides on the rest of this site.
