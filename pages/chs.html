Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome (CHS).

The cyclical vomiting that only hot showers seem to help. The drug that's supposed to stop nausea, causing weeks of nausea. It looks paradoxical, it gets missed for years, and it reverses with cessation.

!

If this is happening to you right now: CHS dehydrates fast. People can develop acute kidney injury and dangerous electrolyte abnormalities. If you're vomiting and can't keep fluids down, go to an emergency department — and tell them you use cannabis. It's not a moral question; it's a diagnostic one.

What it is

Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome is a clinical syndrome of recurrent, severe vomiting in people with long-term, frequent cannabis use, which resolves with cessation. It was first described in 2004 (Allen et al., Gut) and has been increasingly recognized since cannabis legalization expanded access to high-potency products.

It's important up front: this is the part of the cannabis story that confuses everyone the most. THC is famously anti-nauseant at acute doses — it's literally an FDA-approved antiemetic for chemo (dronabinol, nabilone). And yet chronic heavy use produces the opposite effect. That's not contradictory; that's how this class of receptors behaves when you flood them for years.

The three phases

1. Prodromal phase

Often months to years long. Morning nausea, mild abdominal discomfort, fear of vomiting. Many people use more cannabis during this phase, because cannabis acutely reduces nausea and the patient and their friends both assume that's the answer. Weight is usually preserved.

2. Hyperemetic phase

Cyclic, severe, often debilitating vomiting — sometimes dozens of times per day, sometimes for hours on end. Diffuse abdominal pain. Profuse sweating. Compulsive hot showering or bathing, sometimes for hours, sometimes scalding (this is the most distinctive feature). Episodes usually last 1–2 days, separated by symptom-free weeks. Over time, the cycles get closer together and worse.

3. Recovery phase

After complete cessation of cannabis. Vomiting stops. The hot-shower compulsion disappears. Appetite and weight return. Crucially: this can take days to weeks, and people often resume cannabis use during the recovery phase because they "feel fine now." The syndrome then returns, usually faster and worse, within weeks to months.

Why hot showers?

This is the most diagnostically useful feature, and it puzzles people. Patients with CHS will take 5, 8, 12 hot showers a day. They'll sit in a hot bath until the water turns cold, refill it, and sit again. They scald their skin. Family members notice the water bill before they notice the diagnosis.

The leading mechanistic theory: heat activates TRPV1 receptors in the skin and gut — the same receptors that respond to capsaicin (chili peppers) — and TRPV1 modulates the nausea pathway in a way that suppresses vomiting. THC also acts on TRPV1, and chronic THC exposure appears to dysregulate this system. Hot water is the patient's body finding the only switch that works.

This is also why topical capsaicin cream applied to the abdomen has become a standard ED treatment for acute CHS — it activates the same pathway as the hot shower.

Diagnostic features (Rome IV)

All of the following must be present:

  • Stereotypical episodic vomiting resembling cyclic vomiting syndrome in terms of onset, duration, and frequency.
  • Presentation after prolonged, excessive cannabis use.
  • Relief of vomiting episodes by sustained cessation of cannabis use.

Supportive features:

  • Pathological bathing behavior — prolonged hot showers or baths.
  • Onset usually after years of weekly or more frequent use.
  • Predominantly young adults, but increasingly seen at all ages.

What it is not

CHS gets missed because it looks like other things. Most patients see multiple specialists, undergo extensive workups, and are told they have something else first. Common wrong-turn diagnoses:

  • Cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS). CHS and CVS look identical, but CVS is independent of cannabis. The distinguishing question: does it resolve with abstinence?
  • Gastroparesis. Different pattern (constant nausea, not cyclic), but easy to confuse.
  • Functional dyspepsia / IBS. Doesn't explain the vomiting severity.
  • Anxiety or panic disorder. Heat-seeking and bathing rituals are sometimes mislabeled as compulsive behavior.
  • Pregnancy-related hyperemesis. CHS occurs in men and non-pregnant women too, and the bathing pattern is distinctive.

"But I've used cannabis for years and it helps my nausea."

Both can be true at different points in time. Cannabis use can acutely reduce nausea while chronically producing CHS — these are different mechanisms, in different tissues, with different time courses. The acute antiemetic effect comes from central CB1 activation. CHS comes from years of CB1/TRPV1 system dysregulation in the gut and brainstem.

Said another way: every dose of cannabis is two things at once — an acute antiemetic, and an investment in CHS risk. For most users, the acute effect dominates for a long time. For some users, the second one catches up.

Acute management (what an ED will do)

  • IV fluids and electrolytes. Hypokalemia, hypochloremia, and acute kidney injury are common.
  • Topical capsaicin cream applied to the abdomen — surprisingly effective.
  • Haloperidol (low dose IV) — has the best evidence of any anti-emetic for CHS in the ED setting.
  • Benzodiazepines (often lorazepam) for the anxiety/agitation that accompanies episodes.
  • Ondansetron is often used but works poorly compared to the above.
  • A hot shower if the patient can stand it.

None of these solve CHS. They get you through an episode. The only cure is cessation.

What recovery looks like

  • Days 1–7 Symptoms continue, then taper.

    Nausea and vomiting can persist for days after the last use. THC has a long half-life in chronic users — the cause doesn't leave the body immediately.
  • Weeks 1–4 Episodes stop.

    The hot-shower compulsion fades. Appetite returns. Weight comes back. Some patients still have intermittent nausea for a few weeks.
  • Months 1–3 Sustained recovery.

    If cannabis use stays at zero, episodes do not recur. The TRPV1/CB1 system gradually re-regulates.
  • If use resumes The syndrome usually comes back, often worse.

    Even occasional use can re-trigger CHS in someone who's had it. This isn't moral — it's how the receptor system works.

Common questions

Can CBD-only products trigger CHS?

No clear evidence that pure CBD triggers CHS. The syndrome has only been described with THC-containing products. CBD-isolate products are not part of the typical exposure history.

Will cutting down — not quitting — fix it?

In most cases, no. The handful of case series that have looked at this find that reduction without full cessation does not reliably stop the syndrome, and most patients eventually escalate again. The cure is abstinence.

How long after my last dose can CHS still happen?

Vomiting episodes have been reported up to a couple of weeks after stopping in chronic heavy users, then resolve. THC is highly lipophilic and stored in fat — it leaves the system slowly. This delayed clearance is one reason people misattribute their recovery to other factors.

I'm a long-term user but I've never had CHS. Will I get it?

Most long-term users do not develop CHS. The risk factors aren't fully mapped — it appears to involve a combination of dose, duration, genetics, and possibly product type (concentrates seem overrepresented). The honest answer is: we don't know who gets it until they do.
