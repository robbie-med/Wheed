Withdrawal

Yes, cannabis withdrawal is real.

This is probably the single most under-recognized thing about regular cannabis use. It's a defined clinical syndrome (DSM-5, ICD-11), it's been reproduced in controlled inpatient studies, and most daily users will experience some version of it when they stop.

"I've quit cold turkey before. I had no symptoms."

"It's not addictive. There's no physical withdrawal."

"I just smoke to sleep — it's not like I need it."

These are some of the most common things clinicians hear. They aren't lies. They're often the result of one of three things: (1) the person never actually went long enough without to feel it, (2) they did, but attributed the symptoms to something else — work stress, a bad week, "just feeling off" — or (3) they were a light or intermittent user whose dependence really was modest. The first two are far more common than the third.

The DSM-5-TR criteria

Cannabis withdrawal was formally added to the DSM in 2013 (DSM-5). Diagnosis requires abrupt cessation of heavy, prolonged use, plus at least three of the following within about a week:

  1. Irritability, anger, or aggression. "Snapping" at people, feeling on edge.
  2. Nervousness or anxiety. Often the most prominent symptom.
  3. Sleep disturbance. Insomnia, fragmented sleep, vivid or disturbing dreams.
  4. Decreased appetite or weight loss.
  5. Restlessness.
  6. Depressed mood.
  7. At least one physical symptom causing significant discomfort: abdominal pain, shakiness or tremors, sweating, fever, chills, or headache.

DSM-5-TR, American Psychiatric Association, 2022.

The course of withdrawal

  • Hours 6–24 The "off" feeling.

    Subtle irritability and unease. Many people don't recognize this as withdrawal — it just feels like a bad mood or a stressful day.
  • Days 1–2 Sleep falls apart, anxiety rises.

    Falling asleep is hard. Appetite drops. Some people develop sweating, mild tremor, and headache. Mood is reactive.
  • Days 2–6 Peak.

    This is when symptoms are most intense. Vivid dreams and nightmares are common — REM sleep, which cannabis suppresses, comes back hard. GI symptoms (nausea, cramping, loose stools) are not unusual for heavy users.
  • Week 2 Improving, but not done.

    Most physical symptoms have eased. Mood and sleep are still off in many people.
  • Weeks 3–6 Sleep is the last thing to normalize.

    Sleep architecture (REM, deep sleep) gradually returns. Vivid dreams can persist for weeks.
  • Weeks 4+ Protracted symptoms in a minority.

    Some heavy long-term users have residual mood and sleep changes for 1–3 months. These resolve with time.

Course based on Budney AJ et al., American Journal of Psychiatry 2004; Allsop DJ et al., PLoS ONE 2012; Bonnet U & Preuss UW, Substance Abuse and Rehabilitation 2017.

Why people miss it in themselves

The symptoms look like "just life"

Insomnia, irritability, anxiety, low appetite, low mood — these are nonspecific. Most adults can attribute them to something on any given week. If you're not looking for cannabis withdrawal as an explanation, the brain happily provides another one.

Most attempts at quitting are short

If you stop Friday night, feel awful Saturday and Sunday, and use again Monday — you may have truncated the worst of withdrawal without ever realizing you were in it. The body has a long half-life for THC (days to weeks in chronic users), so symptoms can be slow to start.

Tolerance hides dependence

People often think dependence means "I crave it constantly." Most cannabis dependence is quieter: the drug has become how you sleep, how you decompress, how you eat dinner. Removing it isn't a crisis — it's a slow, broad-spectrum deterioration of how the day feels.

Restarting use abolishes the symptoms in minutes

This is the cleanest diagnostic feature: cannabis withdrawal is fully relieved by cannabis. If insomnia, irritability, and night sweats vanish in 10 minutes after a dose, that's not coincidence — that's a withdrawal syndrome.

Who gets withdrawal, and how badly

Use patternLikelihood of clinically significant withdrawal
Occasional (weekends only, low potency)Low
Daily flower, 1–2 sessionsModerate
Multiple times daily, any productHigh
Daily concentrate / dab / high-THC vapeVery high; often more severe
"Wake-and-bake" or use within 1 h of wakingVery high

In a large meta-analysis (Bahji et al., JAMA Network Open 2020), roughly half of people with regular cannabis use developed withdrawal symptoms on cessation. Among people specifically seeking treatment for cannabis use, the prevalence is closer to 70–95%.

"But it's not as bad as alcohol or opioid withdrawal."

Correct. Cannabis withdrawal is not life-threatening. There's no equivalent of delirium tremens. You do not need medical supervision to stop, and you cannot die from stopping.

That doesn't mean it's nothing. Several lines of evidence show cannabis withdrawal is clinically significant:

  • It reliably drives relapse. Most people who try to quit and fail cite the symptoms above — sleep is the most common — as the reason they restarted.
  • It is reproducible in inpatient studies with no expectation effects: people who don't know they're being studied for withdrawal still show the same syndrome (Budney 2003).
  • It has a specific neurobiological basis: CB1 receptor downregulation reverses on cessation, and PET imaging shows this in real time (Hirvonen et al., Molecular Psychiatry 2012).

What helps

Most cannabis withdrawal can be managed without prescription medication. Concrete things that help:

  • Sleep hygiene, fiercely. Consistent wake time matters more than bedtime. Cool, dark, no screens late. Accept that sleep will be poor for 2–3 weeks.
  • Exercise. Even 20–30 minutes of moderate aerobic activity reliably helps mood and sleep during withdrawal (Buchowski et al., PLoS ONE 2011).
  • Hydration and regular meals. Appetite is low; eat anyway. Protein and fiber over sugar.
  • Avoid stacking other depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines) to "take the edge off" — this often becomes the new problem.
  • Tell someone. A friend, partner, or clinician who knows you're cutting back will not solve the symptoms, but accountability and outside perspective help with the irritability piece especially.

If sleep is severe or anxiety is dangerous, talk to a clinician. Short courses of gabapentin or N-acetylcysteine have RCT-level evidence for cannabis use disorder; some clinicians also prescribe non-benzodiazepine sleep aids short-term.
