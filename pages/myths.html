Common claims

Things people commonly say about their own cannabis use.

Not "myths to debunk" — most of these claims contain a kernel of truth, which is why they're so common. Here's where each one is right, where it's incomplete, and what the evidence actually shows.

"I've quit cold turkey before. I had no withdrawal."

Probably partly true and partly missed. A few possibilities:

  • You restarted before symptoms peaked. Heavy-use withdrawal peaks at day 2–6. Many "quits" don't make it that long.
  • You attributed symptoms to something else — work stress, a bad mood, a rough week.
  • You're a light or intermittent user whose dependence really is modest. (Possible — but uncommon if you use multiple times daily.)
  • You had less withdrawal than average. There's significant person-to-person variation, partly genetic.

The cleanest test: pick a calendar date for a 14-day break, write down each day's sleep, mood, appetite, and irritability, and review at the end. If "nothing happens," that's reassuring data. If days 3–6 are noticeably worse than the others — that's withdrawal, with a name.

"It's not addictive — there's no physical withdrawal."

Not true on either count. Cannabis use disorder is in the DSM-5 with formal diagnostic criteria. Roughly 1 in 10 ever-users develop it; ~1 in 6 for adolescent-onset users; higher for daily concentrate users. Physical withdrawal is well-characterized and reproducible in controlled inpatient studies.

What's true: cannabis is less physically dangerous to withdraw from than alcohol or benzodiazepines. You won't seize and you can't die from stopping. But "less dangerous than alcohol withdrawal" is a pretty low bar.

"It's just a plant — natural, so safe."

The plant framing is misleading for the modern product. Tobacco is a plant. Foxglove is a plant. "Natural" isn't a safety category. More importantly, today's commercial cannabis isn't really "the plant" — it's been bred for THC concentrations far above what grew in the wild, and concentrates and distillates are industrial extracts with potency unimaginable in any pre-1990 sample.

Even setting that aside: nothing about a molecule binding CB1 receptors gets safer because the molecule originated in a plant.

"It's safer than alcohol."

True for most acute measures, less clean for chronic ones. Cannabis appears to have essentially no documented fatal overdoses from acute use of the drug alone, and lower acute risk than alcohol for liver, cardiovascular, and many neurological outcomes. On those measures, the comparison holds.

But it doesn't follow that cannabis is "safe." Different drugs cause different problems. Alcohol causes liver disease and motor vehicle deaths; cannabis causes CHS, contributes to psychotic disorders, and is more likely to interfere with cognition and motivation chronically. "Safer than alcohol" is true on average and doesn't tell you what cannabis is doing in your specific life.

"It helps my anxiety. I'd be worse without it."

Acutely true; chronically often inverted. Low-dose THC is anxiolytic in the moment. But chronic daily use is associated with higher rates of anxiety disorders over time, and most of the "I need it for my anxiety" baseline is actually withdrawal anxiety between doses.

The test: anxiety 3–4 weeks after stopping. If it's worse, you have a baseline anxiety disorder that deserves its own treatment. If it's better, much of what you were treating was the drug itself.

"I use it for sleep. It's the only thing that works."

Real short-term effect, narrower long-term picture. Cannabis shortens sleep latency and reduces awakenings — but suppresses REM and produces tolerance, so after a few weeks/months you sleep less and worse than you would without it, while believing you sleep better.

"The only thing that works" usually means "the only thing I've tried recently." CBT-I has larger effect sizes than any sleep medication, including cannabis, and the effects persist after treatment ends. See the sleep page.

"I only smoke flower, not concentrates — so I'm low-risk."

Partly true. Flower is lower risk per unit time than concentrates — but today's flower is 5–7× as potent as 1990s flower. "Just flower" in 2026 is a stronger exposure than concentrate use would have been a generation ago. Risk scales with total THC exposure (dose × frequency × duration), not the product format alone.

"I don't get high anymore — so it's not affecting me."

Backwards. "Not getting high anymore" is the textbook sign of tolerance, which is the textbook sign of dependence. The drug is doing more to your brain, not less. You're just no longer feeling the subjective surge — you're maintaining baseline.

"My grandmother used cannabis until she was 95 and was fine."

Possible and uninformative. Most people who use cannabis are "fine" by gross measures. The question is whether your specific pattern, with today's products, in your specific situation, is doing what you want it to do. Anecdotes about other people's outcomes — good or bad — don't answer that.

"Driving high is fine — I'm a better driver high."

Not supported by the evidence. Cannabis approximately doubles crash risk (Asbridge et al., BMJ 2012 meta-analysis). It impairs reaction time, lane maintenance, and divided attention. The driver's perception of "I'm careful" is consistent across studies but doesn't translate to actual driving performance.

The combined risk of cannabis + alcohol is substantially higher than either alone, and is well above the level where most people would consider driving acceptable.

"My doctor doesn't understand — they're just anti-weed."

Sometimes true. Often not. Most clinicians under 50 have no personal animus toward cannabis. What they have is access to the same patient population over and over — and they've watched a lot of CHS go undiagnosed for years, watched anxiety get worse instead of better, watched motivation and memory drift. When a clinician raises cannabis as a possible contributor, it's almost never moral. It's pattern recognition.

The good faith conversation is: "What pattern are you seeing? What would you want me to try?" — not "you're biased." A clinician who knows you and has read the literature is one of the most valuable resources you have access to.

"It's been making my vomiting worse." vs. "Cannabis treats nausea — that can't be it."

Both can be true, at different time scales. Acute THC is anti-nauseant. Chronic heavy THC use can produce cannabis hyperemesis syndrome, which is debilitating cyclic vomiting that only resolves with cessation. See the CHS page — this is one of the most-missed diagnoses in primary care and emergency medicine.

"If it were really that bad, it wouldn't be legal."

Legality and safety are unrelated. Alcohol, tobacco, and sugar are all legal and all cause more deaths than most illicit drugs. Cannabis legalization has been driven by reasonable arguments about criminal justice, regulation, and personal freedom — not by a finding that cannabis is harmless. The medical literature on cannabis has continued to mature in parallel with legalization, and most of it is sobering.
