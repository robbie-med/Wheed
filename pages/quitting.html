If you want to cut back

A practical plan for cutting down or quitting.

Not a recovery program. Not a 12-step framework. Just a concrete, evidence-based set of approaches for someone who's decided to change their use — for any reason, by any amount, on any timeline.

First: get clear on what you want

"Quitting cannabis" can mean any of several things, and which one you're aiming at changes the plan:

GoalUseful framing
Permanent abstinenceThe cleanest outcome biologically. Removes withdrawal-driven anxiety and sleep problems from your baseline.
Tolerance break (2–4 weeks)Resets receptors. Restores the high. Useful for evaluating "what's me vs. what's the drug" without committing to anything permanent.
Cutting back to occasional useHarder than abstinence for most heavy users — sustained moderation is uncommon — but achievable, especially if you change what you use (e.g., low-potency flower instead of concentrates).
Changing one specific pattern"No use before X PM," "no use at work," "no use alone." Surprisingly effective starting points if abstinence feels overwhelming.

None of these are better than the others morally. They are different goals with different mechanics.

Pick your approach

Option A: Cold turkey

Most heavy users actually have more success with abrupt cessation than gradual tapering — there's no reservoir of cannabis around the house, the worst withdrawal is compressed into the first week instead of dragged out, and the question "should I have some?" gets answered once instead of every day.

Downsides: the first week is unpleasant. Sleep especially is bad. You need to plan for it.

Option B: Taper

If concentrates or high-potency products are your main use, stepping down to lower potency before stopping can ease the transition. A reasonable schedule:

  • Week 1: cut frequency in half (e.g., from 4 uses/day to 2)
  • Week 2: cut frequency in half again, or switch to lower-potency flower
  • Week 3: once-daily evening use, lowest dose that works
  • Week 4: stop

Tapering works for some people. For others it just prolongs the pain. If you've tried tapering twice and it hasn't gotten you to your goal, cold turkey is usually the faster path.

Option C: Substitution (be careful)

Some people swap cannabis for alcohol, benzodiazepines, or kratom during the withdrawal period. This is one of the most common ways a cannabis problem becomes a worse problem. If you're going to use anything to ease the transition, do it under clinician guidance and on a strict timeline.

What to plan for, day by day

  • Day 1 The easy day.

    Most people feel OK or even slightly euphoric ("I'm doing this!"). Symptoms haven't hit yet because THC is still in the system.
  • Days 2–3 Sleep crashes; mood drops.

    Insomnia starts. Irritability rises. Appetite drops. Many people sweat at night. This is the hardest stretch for sleep specifically — plan for poor sleep, not for it not happening.
  • Days 3–7 Peak symptoms.

    If you're going to feel awful, this is when. Anxiety, restlessness, vivid dreams, sometimes nausea or GI upset. The temptation to "just take a hit" is highest here. Push through.
  • Days 7–14 Improving, unevenly.

    Physical symptoms ease. Mood is still reactive. Sleep is still off but trending better. You may notice your appetite, dreams, and concentration in interesting ways.
  • Weeks 2–4 New baseline.

    This is when most people start to feel "themselves" again — often better than they remember. Sleep architecture is restoring. CB1 receptors are repopulating. The drug's contribution to your baseline is no longer there to mask or distort.
  • Months 1–3 The honesty period.

    Now you can see what's underneath. If there's a depression, an anxiety disorder, an unresolved trauma, or a sleep problem that the cannabis was masking — it's visible now, treatable now. This is often when meaningful clinical work becomes possible.

Things that actually help

Sleep planning

Accept that sleep will be bad for 2–3 weeks. Keep a consistent wake time. Don't lie in bed scrolling. Don't add alcohol to fix it. Consider CBT-I (the strongest non-drug treatment for insomnia) starting in week 2.

Exercise

20–40 minutes of moderate aerobic exercise has been shown to reduce withdrawal symptoms in a small study (Buchowski 2011). Doesn't have to be intense. Walking counts.

Pick a start date and tell one person

Accountability without judgment. A friend, partner, or clinician who knows you're cutting back — they don't need to police you, just to know.

Remove the supply

Don't keep a "just in case" stash. The hardest decision is in the moment, and you make a better decision when the moment doesn't include reaching for a vape on the nightstand.

Pre-cook some meals

Appetite drops sharply in withdrawal. Cooking is harder when you feel awful. Have easy, palatable food on hand — soup, fruit, whatever you can eat without thinking.

Track your symptoms

Even a one-line daily note is useful. It lets you see the curve instead of just the worst moment. The withdrawal check can be repeated daily.

Medications, if you want them

You don't need medication to stop using cannabis, and most people who succeed don't use any. No drug is FDA-approved specifically for cannabis use disorder, but several have been studied with mixed results:

  • N-acetylcysteine (NAC) — over-the-counter supplement. A positive adolescent trial (Gray et al., American Journal of Psychiatry 2012) was not replicated in a larger adult trial (Gray et al., Drug and Alcohol Dependence 2017). Possible benefit mainly in adolescents and young adults; not clearly effective in adults.
  • Gabapentin — short-course prescription; a small RCT showed reduced craving and improved sleep/anxiety in cannabis-dependent adults (Mason et al., Neuropsychopharmacology 2012). Evidence is preliminary.
  • Mirtazapine, trazodone, or similar — sometimes prescribed short-term for the worst nights of withdrawal insomnia.
  • Naltrexone, topiramate, and others — limited or mixed trial evidence; not first-line.

None of these are required. All of them are tools your clinician can offer. There's no "medication-assisted treatment" for cannabis use disorder in the way there is for opioids — this is a behavioral problem with pharmacological support, not the other way around.

If you slip

A slip is not a failure. The data is consistent across substances: most quit attempts involve at least one slip. The patients who do well are the ones who treat slips as data ("what was the trigger? what's different about that situation?") instead of verdicts ("I'm hopeless").

The thing to avoid is the "well, I already broke it" spiral — where one slip becomes a full return to use, then a new baseline. The single behavior that distinguishes successful quit attempts from unsuccessful ones is treating a slip as a slip and going back to abstinence the next day.

When to involve a professional

  • You've tried to cut down twice and not succeeded.
  • You have an existing mental health condition (anxiety, depression, bipolar, psychosis, eating disorder, PTSD).
  • You're using other substances and need to coordinate.
  • Withdrawal symptoms are severe or scary.
  • You're not sure whether what you're feeling is withdrawal or an underlying problem.
  • You just want help. That's a sufficient reason.

Cannabis use disorder responds well to brief interventions, motivational interviewing, and CBT — usually 6–12 sessions, sometimes fewer. Most clinicians who treat substance use disorders also treat cannabis. See the resources page.
