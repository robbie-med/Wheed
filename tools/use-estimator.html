Use & cost estimator

Most people genuinely don't know how much cannabis they're consuming or what it costs them annually. This tool gives you a number. Everything stays in your browser.

You can fill in whichever product you mostly use — flower, vape cart, concentrate, or edibles. The numbers update as you type. If you use more than one, run it twice and add the results in your head, or pick the one that dominates.

A typical joint is 0.3–0.5 g. A bowl is ~0.25 g. A heavy daily user is 1–2 g.

Dispensary flower is typically 15–30%. If you don't know, 20% is a good estimate.

US dispensary average for flower is ~$10/g. Concentrates run $30–60/g; vape carts ~$25–50 each.

How to read this

The "THC per day" number is the total chemical THC content of what you consume — not the amount that reaches your bloodstream. Bioavailability varies: smoking and vaping deliver about 20–35% to the bloodstream, edibles about 4–20%. The total content number is still useful because it scales linearly with exposure to the receptor system that drives tolerance, dependence, and CHS risk.

For context: a typical joint in the early 1990s contained roughly 10 mg of THC content. That's a reference point — not a target. A current daily user of dispensary flower is usually in the hundreds of mg per day. A daily concentrate user is often in the thousands.

About the cost number

The annual cost figure surprises a lot of people. Common patterns:

  • "$10–15 a day in flower" → $3,600–5,500 per year
  • "A vape cart a week" → $1,300–2,500 per year
  • "A gram of concentrate every couple days" → $5,000–11,000 per year

This is not a moral observation — money spent on cannabis is no different from money spent on alcohol, eating out, or any other discretionary category. But many users have never added it up and find the multi-year number more motivating than any health argument.

What the "30 days off" number isn't

It's not pressure. Lots of people read it, think "huh, interesting," and keep using. That's a fine outcome — the tool's job is to show you what's true, not to tell you what to do.

It's also not a guarantee that 30 days off would be easy. See the withdrawal page for what to expect, and the quitting page for how to actually do it.
