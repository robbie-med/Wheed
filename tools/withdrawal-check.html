Cannabis Withdrawal Scale

These are the 19 symptoms in the Cannabis Withdrawal Scale (Allsop et al., 2012), the most-cited measure of cannabis withdrawal in research. Rate each symptom from 0 (not at all) to 10 (extremely) over the past 24 hours. Nothing is stored or sent.

Important framing: the 19 items and 0–10 scoring below are from the published CWS. The interpretation of any specific total score is not standardized — the original validation did not define hard severity cutoffs. This page reports your total and offers descriptive context based on the literature, but it is an educational self-rating, not a validated diagnostic instrument. A clinician's evaluation is the only way to actually diagnose cannabis withdrawal.

For each symptom, drag the slider to where it's been for you over the past 24 hours. 0 = not at all · 10 = extremely.

How to read this

The CWS total ranges from 0 to 190 (19 items × 0–10 each). The "Discomfort Score" is the sum of all item ratings. The mean per item (total ÷ 19) gives a rough sense of average symptom intensity.

The original Allsop 2012 paper and subsequent work using the CWS did not establish fixed severity cutoffs. The scale's intended use is longitudinal tracking — rating the same person across days to see whether symptoms are intensifying, peaking, or resolving — and as a continuous outcome variable in trials, not as a single-time diagnostic threshold.

What the literature does support:

  • In dependent cannabis users following cessation, total scores typically rise from day 1, peak around days 2–6, and decline through weeks 2–3.
  • Mean total scores in research samples vary widely; peak medians reported are typically in the 20–60 range, with substantial individual variation. Some heavy users score much higher; many lighter users score much lower.
  • The most discriminating items in Allsop's validation were craving, sleep difficulty, mood-related items (anxiety, irritability, depression), and appetite loss. A score driven mostly by these has a more characteristic pattern than the same score driven by, say, headache alone.

Interpreting your number, descriptively

Rather than invented severity bands, here are honest interpretive frames:

  • If your score is low and you've recently stopped: you may not be at peak yet (the curve usually rises through days 2–6), or you may not have been a heavy enough user to produce a strong syndrome. Either is reassuring.
  • If your score is moderate-to-high and you're days into a stop: you're experiencing the syndrome the way moderate-to-heavy users typically do. Uncomfortable, not dangerous, and following an expected curve. The quitting page has practical strategies; the single most important one is making it past day 6 without resuming use.
  • If your score is high and you're still using: this is unusual — withdrawal items, by definition, ease with use. If you're scoring high while actively using, it's worth asking whether what you're rating is really withdrawal or whether something else is going on (anxiety disorder, depression, illness).
  • If your score is moderate or high and you're more than 4 weeks out: classic cannabis withdrawal should be mostly resolved by then. Lingering symptoms at that timepoint often have another contributor worth investigating — talk to a clinician.

What this is and isn't

This is a way to put a number on something. It is not a diagnosis of cannabis withdrawal — the DSM-5-TR criteria (≥3 specific symptoms in a recent abstainer from heavy use) are the diagnostic standard, and a clinician applies them. Use this score the way you'd use a daily mood rating: as data, repeated over time, to see your own curve.

Reference

Allsop DJ, Norberg MM, Copeland J, Fu S, Budney AJ. The Cannabis Withdrawal Scale development: patterns and predictors of cannabis withdrawal and distress. PLoS ONE. 2012;7(9):e44864.
